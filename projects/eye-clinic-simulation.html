Cutting an Eye Clinic's Wait Time Without Cutting Its Margin

Basir Eye Clinic runs about 8,000 patient visits a month through one corridor — new patients, post-operative reviews and returning cases all sharing the same reception, cashier, imaging and doctor stations. Management's own read on patient complaints was clear: waiting time, not price or outcome, was the top dissatisfaction driver. I built a validated Arena simulation of the clinic, tested thirteen candidate fixes against it, and ranked them with TOPSIS on time, staff workload and profit together — not on wait time alone.

What this is. A discrete-event simulation calibrated to the clinic's own 2016 operating data (input distributions fitted in Arena's Input Analyzer, p > 0.05) and validated against real patient records — simulated average time in system for each of the three patient types landed within a few per cent of the observed values. Thirteen improvement scenarios were run through the same model and scored with TOPSIS across time in system, five station utilisation rates and a daily-profit objective. The figures below are read directly from that model's output and the TOPSIS scoring workbook, not estimated.

One corridor, three kinds of patient

About 326 people a day, each following a different path through the same rooms.

Basir has run as a private specialist eye clinic since the early 1990s and was the first centre in the region to install excimer laser refractive correction — for years one of only two in the country. Every patient still passes through some combination of the same stations: reception, cashier, computerised vision testing, optometry, and the doctor, with a side trip to paraclinic imaging for those who need it.

Patients arrive as three distinct populations, not one queue: about half are visiting for the first time, around a third are returning cases whose paper file has to be pulled from the archive before they can be seen, and roughly one in seven are post-operative patients back for a next-day review. Arrivals themselves are uneven — light from 7am, peaking hard around 9am as everyone tries to beat the day's doctor appointments filling up.

Paraclinic imaging carries the single longest queue, but it's downstream of the doctor and only a subset of patients pass through it. The doctor sits on every patient's path without exception, at 93% utilisation with next to no slack — which is exactly why, once all thirteen fixes were actually tested against the model rather than guessed at, doctor capacity is what moved the outcome.

Thirteen fixes, ranked by TOPSIS — not by wait time alone

Nine resource-increase scenarios, four process fixes, scored on time in system, five station workloads and daily profit together.

Adding staff anywhere is easy to propose and easy to over-do. The scenarios tested ranged from more doctors, cashiers, reception clerks and optometrists, to process changes: digitising the paper archive, merging the cashier and reception steps, adding a triage step to catch patients who don't need a doctor visit, and smoothing appointment bookings across the day. Each was run through the same validated model, then scored with TOPSIS against a stated ideal — shortest time in system, a healthy-not-punishing utilisation band for every station, and the highest daily profit — so a scenario that only wins on one dimension doesn't automatically win overall.

Notice the fourth row: the single most profitable scenario tested wasn't the fastest one, and it wasn't the TOPSIS winner either — it ranked 4th once queue time and every station's workload were weighed in alongside profit. That's the whole point of scoring on more than one axis: the best-looking number in isolation isn't always the best decision.

The two top-ranked scenarios share the same signature in the model's output: doctor utilisation drops sharply while every other station's workload barely moves — the clear fingerprint of added doctor capacity, not a process tweak. The winning scenario pushes further than its near-twin runner-up and, tellingly, is also the only one of the two that ends up more profitable than doing nothing: it doesn't just clear the backlog, it lets the clinic complete more patient visits in the same day, and that extra completed volume is what pays for the added desks.

That's the number that actually matters to a clinic owner deciding whether to hire: staffing up the bottleneck resource here isn't a cost the business absorbs for the sake of patient satisfaction — on this model, it pays for itself.
