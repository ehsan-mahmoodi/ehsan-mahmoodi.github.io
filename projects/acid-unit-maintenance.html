Simulation & Optimization Case Study

Maintenance Policy Planning and Optimization — Zinc Smelting Complex, Acid Production Unit

A zinc smelting complex runs a sulfuric acid plant as a second production line, not a side project: the acid separates roasted ore from its oxide, and the unit's byproduct steam supplies heat to the rest of the site for free. When a blower or pump on that unit fails, the complex loses both. The question was which maintenance policy — and which repair crew size — actually minimises downtime and cost, tested by simulating a full year of failures under three competing policies rather than guessing from intuition.

Stakeholder engagement · business problem

Why the acid unit, and why now

2013, Bafgh Zinc Smelting Complex, Iran — an MSc thesis engagement in industrial engineering.

The complex's sulfuric acid factory was built in 1999 to a nominal 150 t/day at 98% H₂SO₄, consuming roughly 40 t/day of the sulfur produced when roasting zinc ore. Two prior shutdown episodes, from corrosion in lines and joints, had already forced a major overhaul; the plant was ramping back toward nameplate capacity when this study started. The engagement was with the factory's maintenance team and its 20-person cross-trade repair crew, and the business problem was concrete: unplanned failures of the unit's blowers and pumps were stopping acid production, which in turn stalls the zinc roasting line and forfeits the plant's free byproduct steam — and every shutdown-restart cycle burns roughly 7,000 litres of gas-oil reheating the furnace and accelerates wear on the same rotating equipment that just failed.

Data & operations

The process, and what keeps it running

From sulfur burner to stored acid, with the equipment that has to stay up.
  1. Sulfur burning. Molten sulfur, sprayed by pumps into a furnace fed with dried combustion air, burns to SO₂ at around 900°C.
  2. Catalytic conversion. SO₂ passes through a four-bed converter over vanadium pentoxide catalyst, oxidising to SO₃; the reaction heat is recovered as steam.
  3. Absorption. SO₃ is absorbed into recirculating acid and diluted with water to 98% H₂SO₄, then sent to 3,000-tonne storage tanks.
  4. Byproduct steam. The recovered steam (about 10 t/h at 7 barg, 180°C) is the plant's one free heat source — used elsewhere in the complex at no fuel cost, so long as the unit is running.
  5. Rotating equipment. Blowers move combustion air and process gas; pumps circulate sulfur, acid and water. These, not the static vessels, are what actually fail.
  6. Shared repair crew. One 20-person team — six mechanics, four piping, four welders, three electrical, two instrument, one civil — covers the whole complex, not just this unit, so a repair here competes with repairs everywhere else.

Failure and repair data were collected on-site over a one-year review window: blower failures follow an exponential distribution with an 80-day mean time between failures, pump failures a 30-day mean; repair time and cost both depend on whether the crew fixes the unit in place, brings it into the factory workshop, or sends it out for external repair.

Descriptive & diagnostic analysis

What the current policy actually costs

Repair only after failure, with no early warning.

The policy in place at the time was pure corrective maintenance: fix it when it breaks, with the crew sizes already on staff. Descriptively, that policy loses the acid unit roughly 3,151 hours of production a year — about 36% of the calendar year offline — to breakdowns and repairs, at a maintenance cost of some 225 million Toman a month. Diagnostically, the reason isn't crew size on its own — it's that the policy has no mechanism to catch a failing blower or pump before it stops the line, and every stoppage cascades: the unit shuts down, the converter beds have to be purged of gas rather than left to cool a valuable catalyst charge, and restart burns fuel and time that a policy with any warning at all would avoid.

Predictive modelling · backend

Simulating a year under three policies

Discrete-event simulation of failure, diagnosis and repair, for each policy in turn.

The predictive layer is a discrete-event simulation, built in Arena, of the blower and pump failure-repair cycle competing for the same shared crew as the rest of the complex. Three maintenance policies were modelled and run forward, each replicated 30 times to stabilise the downtime and cost estimates:

  • Corrective (breakdown). Repair only after the equipment stops — the current policy.
  • Preventive. Scheduled weekly inspections of every blower and pump, with a probability of triggering immediate, scheduled or deferred repair depending on what the inspection finds, on top of whatever random failures still occur between inspections.
  • Predictive (condition-based). Continuous monitoring of vibration, noise and temperature; when a symptom appears, the model estimates remaining time to failure and triggers an emergency repair if that's under 24 hours, or a scheduled one if there's more runway.

Each policy was tested at the current crew sizes and produced its own downtime and cost curve before any staffing changes — the predictive policy alone, with no other change, already cuts annual downtime from around 3,151 hours to under 90.

Prescriptive optimisation · backend

Sizing the crew, then ranking 18 scenarios

OptQuest search per policy, TOPSIS ranking across all of them.

For each of the three policies, OptQuest for Arena searched combinations of crew size across the six trades to minimise a combined objective of downtime and repair cost, producing six candidate scenarios per policy — 18 in total. Those 18 were then ranked together with TOPSIS across eight criteria: monthly maintenance cost, annual downtime, and a desirability score for each trade's utilisation, since a crew that's either idle or constantly overloaded is worse than one that's simply busy.

The policy running in the plant today ranked dead last of the 18 scenarios tested — even the best-staffed breakdown-only configuration still cost roughly seven times more per month than Predictive-06. The winning configuration keeps the crew almost the same size (5 mechanics, 4 welders, 3 piping, 3 instrument, 3 electrical, 1 civil — 19 people against today's 20) and changes only the policy: continuous condition monitoring instead of waiting for a breakdown.

The recommendation isn't a trade-off a manager has to weigh — predictive maintenance with an optimised crew mix beats the current policy on downtime and on cost simultaneously, which is why it topped the ranking outright rather than landing on some Pareto front. Cutting 3,151 hours of annual downtime to 108 gives the plant back roughly four months of production a year, and the condition monitoring itself (vibration, noise, temperature sensing) is a modest capital outlay against a policy that was previously costing 225 million Toman a month and never saw a failure coming.
