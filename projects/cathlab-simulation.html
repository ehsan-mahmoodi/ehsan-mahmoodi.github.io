Simulation & Optimization Demonstrator

A Cath Lab Animation That Was Never Paid For — Built Anyway

In 2014 I mapped a cardiac catheterisation lab's entire patient journey at Moheb-e-Mehr Hospital — every hand-off from admission to discharge, in a full swimlane process chart, with the priority rules staff actually use. The plan was a discrete-event model with a proper animation to show management where the process actually jammed. The hospital wasn't willing to pay for the animation, so it never got built. In 2026 I rebuilt it anyway — in Python, with synthetic data standing in for the measurements that were never funded, and an optimisation layer the original scope never even asked for.

What's real and what's synthetic. The process structure, the three procedure types, the doctor-presence call priority and the monitored-bed rule are all from the real 2014 engagement documents — a swimlane chart of the full patient path and a written list of priority rules. The thirteen service-time measurements the original study planned to collect were never taken (the animation was cut before that stage), so every duration in this model is a synthetic, structurally-reasonable stand-in. The simulation (SimPy), the animation (Python/ Matplotlib) and the NSGA-II bed-configuration optimiser (pymoo) are new, built in 2026 to show what the original scope would have delivered.

The real constraint: one table, two kinds of bed

From the 2014 process chart and priority-rule documentation.

The lab runs one procedure table for three case types: diagnostic angiography, planned angioplasty, and an "ad-hoc" case — a diagnostic that turns into an angioplasty on the table, decided mid-procedure. Patients are called from the ward to the table by a documented priority rule: if their own doctor happens to be in the lab, they go next; otherwise it's first in, first out. The hospital's admission schedule works the same way — there isn't a fixed slot system, so timing depends heavily on which doctor is around.

One rule matters more than the others: angioplasty and ad-hoc patients must recover in a monitored bed, by policy, not by choice. A diagnostic-only patient can recover in ordinary ward capacity, which the hospital has plenty of. Monitored beds are the scarce resource — and if none is free the moment a table case finishes, that patient physically can't be moved, so the table itself sits blocked, unable to start the next case.

The real hospital runs seven wards with different bed counts, mostly shared with other departments. For a model this size that's noise, not signal — so it's simplified to the two pools that actually govern the cath lab's own throughput: pre-cath holding bays, and monitored recovery beds.

What to build: NSGA-II over bed count

Two decision variables, three objectives, the exact Pareto front — confirmed against brute force.

The optimiser searches over two numbers: how many pre-cath bays, and how many monitored recovery beds. Three objectives, all in tension: maximise patients served per day, minimise total beds (the cost proxy), and minimise table-blocking minutes — the expensive resource sitting idle because recovery has nowhere to put the last patient. The search space here is small enough to brute-force exhaustively, so I could check NSGA-II's answer against the true front directly: it found all seven non-dominated configurations, no more and no less.

The finding isn't "add beds" — it's which beds. Two more monitored beds turn a table that spends nearly half an hour blocked per case into one that almost never waits, and lifts daily throughput by 60%. Beyond that point the curve goes flat: going all the way to twelve beds barely serves one more patient a day than five beds does. That's the number a hospital actually needs before it commits capital — not "more capacity", but the specific, cheap fix that unlocks the table.

This is also the argument for building the demonstrator even after the client said no: the model itself is the deliverable. The numbers above are synthetic, but the method — find the resource whose scarcity blocks the expensive one, then optimise around it — is exactly what would have been handed over in 2014 if the animation had been funded.
