Simulation & Optimization Case Study

Where a Cardiac Cath Lab's Day Actually Jams

Moheb-e-Mehr Hospital's catheterisation lab runs one procedure table for three case types, with no fixed admission schedule — patients are called in largely by which cardiologist happens to be in the building. Management's question was simple and expensive: before committing capital to a second table or more ward beds, where does the day actually break down, and what's the smallest fix that clears it? This is the full path from that question to an answer — process mapping, descriptive and diagnostic analysis, a predictive simulation, and a prescriptive optimisation over bed configuration.

Stakeholder engagement · business problem

What the hospital actually needed

2014, Moheb-e-Mehr Hospital, Tehran.

The engagement started with the people who run the lab, not the data: interviews with cath lab and ward staff produced a full swimlane process chart of the patient journey and a written set of the priority rules staff actually follow — which patient gets called next, and which bed a patient is allowed to recover in. The business problem behind the request was a capital one: the hospital was considering a second procedure table or additional monitored beds, and wanted evidence for where the constraint actually was before spending on either.

Data & operations

The process, six stages

From the referral letter to the settled account, as documented on-site.
  1. Pre-admission. Referral letter and existing test results checked at reception before the clinic day starts; incomplete results are sent to the lab.
  2. Laboratory. Bloodwork run or verified if existing results aren't current enough for the procedure.
  3. Admission & payment. Hospital registration and cashier, then a queue for a ward bed. Patients are called by priority: emergency cases first, then whoever's own doctor is currently in the lab, then first in, first out.
  4. Pre-cath ward. A nurse preps the patient — consent, fasting check, IV line — while the ward secretary waits for the cath lab to call for that patient.
  5. CathLab procedure. Vein access confirmed, patient transferred to the one procedure table. Three case types: diagnostic angiography, a planned angioplasty, or an "ad-hoc" case — a diagnostic that converts to an angioplasty mid-procedure, decided on the table.
  6. Post-Cath. Sandbag/compression at the puncture site, recovery, the doctor's discharge order, and settling the account. One clinical rule governs where a patient recovers: angioplasty and ad-hoc patients must go to a monitored bed; diagnostic-only patients can recover in ordinary ward capacity.

The hospital runs seven wards feeding the lab, most shared with other departments and already carrying their own occupancy. For the analysis that matters, they collapse to the two pools that actually govern the cath lab's own throughput: pre-cath holding bays, and monitored recovery beds.

Descriptive & diagnostic analysis

Where the day actually jams

Not the table itself — what happens right after it.

Descriptively, the table is busy roughly the whole clinic day, across a mix of about half diagnostic cases, three in ten planned angioplasties, and the rest ad-hoc conversions. That on its own says the table is the bottleneck — the diagnostic question is why, exactly, and the answer isn't simply "one table."

A finished procedure only frees the table once the patient physically leaves it. For an angioplasty or ad-hoc case that means a monitored bed has to be free at that moment. If it isn't, the patient — and the table — are stuck until one opens up. Diagnostic patients never hit this, because ordinary ward recovery is effectively unconstrained. Monitored beds are the real scarce resource, and their scarcity is what turns an expensive table into an idle one.

Predictive modelling · backend

A model of one day, run forward

Discrete-event simulation of the full six-stage journey, animated.

The predictive layer is a discrete-event simulation of the entire patient path above: arrivals, the priority-based call order, the pre-cath bay, the table, and the split between monitored and general recovery, including the table-blocking behaviour the diagnosis identified. Running it forward under a given bed configuration predicts how a day actually plays out — not just an average, but the queueing and blocking that averages hide.

The process structure and priority rules driving the model are the ones documented on-site; the service-time durations feeding it are modelled from typical departmental ranges rather than a completed timing study, so the absolute minutes should be read as representative, not measured. The relative story — where the blocking comes from and what relieves it — is what the diagnosis above already established independently.

Prescriptive optimisation · backend

What to actually build: NSGA-II over bed count

Two decision variables, three objectives, the exact Pareto front — confirmed against brute force.

The optimisation layer turns the predictive model into a recommendation. It searches over two numbers — pre-cath bays, and monitored recovery beds — against three objectives in tension: maximise patients served per day, minimise total beds (the capital proxy), and minimise table-blocking minutes. The search space here is small enough to brute-force exhaustively, which made it possible to check NSGA-II's answer against the true front directly: it found all seven non-dominated configurations, no more and no less.

The finding isn't "add beds" — it's which beds. Two more monitored beds turn a table that spends nearly half an hour blocked per case into one that almost never waits, and lift daily throughput by 60%. Beyond that point the curve goes flat: twelve beds barely serve one more patient a day than five beds do. That's the number a hospital needs before it commits capital to a second table — the specific, cheap fix that unlocks the one it already has, found by tracing the problem from a stakeholder interview through to an optimised recommendation rather than guessing at "more capacity."
