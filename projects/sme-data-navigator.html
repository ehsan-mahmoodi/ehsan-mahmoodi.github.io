Stakeholder engagement · problem formulation

The interviews changed the brief

Research prototype, Auckland University of Technology, supported by CILT New Zealand.

The project started as a data navigator: a way for small transport and logistics operators to find the New Zealand data sources that could help them. Interviews with operators pointed somewhere we did not expect. Day-to-day data needs are mostly met — most operators already use tracking and planning tools. The harder problems are commercial: rates that do not cover costs, one-sided contracts, and relying on one or two large customers. Better data alone does not fix those.

So the problem was reformulated. The application now does two things:

  1. Helps one operator work out what their work really costs, whether a rate pays, and which contract terms carry the risk.
  2. Pools what everyone learns, anonymously, so the sector can show a pattern that no single operator can prove alone.

Diagnostic analysis

Does this job pay — and why not

An operator’s own costs, set against the rate on offer and the market.

The cost calculator takes truck type, distance, hours, fuel, road user charges, wages and fixed costs, and returns a break-even rate per kilometre and per hour — then says whether the rate on offer covers it. The contract check turns eleven questions into a red, amber or green result and the questions to raise before signing.

In v3.1 the calculator starts from the published diesel price instead of a fixed assumption, and shows the gap between what costs are doing and what freight prices are doing in the operator’s own terms: “that is $12 more per week, $623 a year”. That change alone moved the example operator from losing $341 a week to losing $495 — the old assumption had been flattering.

Data governance · privacy by design

Pooling rates between competitors, legally

Sharing rates between competitors can break the law, so the rules are built into the data model.
  • No sign-up and no names. The app never asks who the operator is or who their customers are.
  • Bands, not exact figures. “Paid in 21–30 days”, not the invoice.
  • Groups of five or more. Nothing is shown for a smaller group, so nobody can be picked out; sector insights stay hidden until at least ten operators have used the app.
  • Past periods only. Shared rates must be from a period that ended at least three months ago — agreeing current or future prices between competitors would be cartel conduct under the Commerce Act.
  • The app never recommends a rate. It shows an operator their own costs, and what others reported for past periods.
  • Data stays in New Zealand, in the Auckland Azure region, which also supports ethics approval. Interview material is kept out of the app entirely.

  • Workshop links. A link such as ?group=sep26 labels everything entered in a session, still anonymously, and one command exports it as spreadsheets for analysis.
  • Sourced reference figures. Diesel and cost-index figures live in a small file with the date and source of each, refreshed by hand — the publishers’ terms do not allow scraping.
Business value · honest limits

What it’s worth

Modest but real for one operator; unique for the sector.

For one operator the value is a floor: knowing the point below which a job is not worth taking, and not signing a bad contract. For the sector, the pooled evidence is the part nobody else has. What the app cannot do is change who holds the power — a take-it-or-leave-it customer stays one — and it cannot promise higher rates.

Still in progress. Next: sit with five to eight operators on a real job and ask one question — did this change a decision you were about to make? — run a workshop to fill the rate comparison, and find an institutional home, measuring decisions changed rather than visits.
