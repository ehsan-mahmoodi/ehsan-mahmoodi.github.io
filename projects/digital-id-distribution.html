Sizing a National Digital ID Rollout, One Centre at a Time

A national digital ID programme is really two systems pretending to be one: a physical service — staff who check documents, take a photo, capture a fingerprint, and hand back a card — and a digital one, where every one of those visits has to land correctly in a central register. Undersize the first and citizens queue for hours. Undersize the second and the queue moves but the record doesn't. In 2013 I modelled the first in detail, station by station in Arena, and used the result to reason about the second: what a rollout to a nation of ninety million people actually demands.

What this is. A discrete-event simulation of one distribution centre's real front-of-house process, run for ten replications of a working day. The throughput and utilisation figures below are the model's own output. The national centre-count and data-traffic estimates further down are back-of-envelope, built on that per-centre result plus one stated assumption — a roughly one-year target to enrol the whole population — flagged wherever it's used.

The process, station by station

Six steps between walking in and walking out with a card.

Applicants don't arrive as one population. Some are enrolling for the first time, some are renewing, some are replacing a lost or damaged card, some need staff assistance — each case carries its own service time. The centre also runs separate queues for men and women, a real operational constraint the model carries through rather than averages away.

Every case still passes through the same stations: a front-desk triage, a check of the request form and supporting documents, a queue number, an operator desk where the photo and fingerprint are captured and the form is scanned, a digital submission to the central register, a status confirmation back, and finally the printed card handed over at collection.

The finding that matters: at a single centre, the national data link looks irrelevant. The people at the front desk are the entire constraint — three operators running at 85–95% utilisation, and a roughly 1-in-20 applicant already walking away before being served, at what is meant to be normal load. Anyone sizing this system by looking at the network first is solving the wrong problem.

That centre count is before renewals, replacements and assisted cases start arriving on top of first-time enrolment — the ongoing demand the index card already flags as its own service stream. It is also exactly the kind of number a national programme needs before it commits to a staffing budget or a site lease, not after.

What that means for the data centre

The backend a single centre made look irrelevant is the one thing that can't fail nationally.

Each applicant touches the central register twice — once to submit the digital form, once to receive the status confirmation back. At one centre that's roughly 200 transactions across a working day, which is why the link sits under 1% busy in the simulation. Multiply by the ~3,600 centres the one-year target implies, running the same shift simultaneously, and it becomes about 720,000 transactions across the day — on the order of 20–25 transactions per second sustained, from thousands of physically separate sites, with real peaks higher than that average.

That is the actual design point for the national infrastructure: not bandwidth to one building, but a register that can absorb continuous, geographically distributed writes without becoming the new bottleneck the moment the front-desk one is solved. A single-centre pilot would never surface that number on its own — it only appears once you multiply by the scale the rollout is actually built for.
