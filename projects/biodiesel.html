Design Case Study

An SME-Scale Biodiesel Plant for a Caspian Cooperative

In 2008 I wrote a bachelor thesis on biodiesel. It was a careful piece of chemistry — acid and base catalysed transesterification, supercritical methanol, enzymatic routes, reaction mechanisms — and it answered a question nobody was asking. It never said who would build the plant, where the oil would come from, or whether the numbers closed. This is that project, done properly.

What this is. A design study, not a built plant. The process configuration and engineering logic are mine; the cost and price figures are stated assumptions drawn from published ranges for plants of this scale, set out openly in the economics section so you can move them. The point of the study is not the payback number — it is showing which single variable that number actually depends on.

Why the Caspian coast, and why small

The siting decision comes before any chemistry.

Biodiesel economics are feedstock economics. Roughly three quarters of the lifetime cost of a plant like this walks in through the gate as oil, which means the only siting question that matters is whether you can get cheap oil reliably, close by, for twenty years.

The Caspian provinces — Gilan, Mazandaran, Golestan — have two things most of Iran does not. First, a dense hospitality and food-processing sector: a tourist coastline, a rice-milling industry, and thousands of restaurants generating used cooking oil that is currently a disposal problem rather than a commodity. Second, a humid, temperate climate suited to rapeseed as a winter crop on land that sits idle between rice harvests, so feedstock oil can be grown without displacing food production.

Small matters too. A national-scale refinery needs a national-scale collection network and a national-scale balance sheet. A 3,000 tonne per year plant can be owned by an agricultural cooperative, fed from a 60 km collection radius, and financed at a scale that an SME can actually raise. It also fails cheaply, which is the honest argument for a pilot.

The choice worth defending here is the acid pre-treatment stage. It adds a vessel and a cost line, and a plant running clean virgin oil would not need it. But the whole economic case rests on cheap used cooking oil, and used cooking oil arrives with free fatty acids that would otherwise turn catalyst and product into soap. The pre-treatment stage is what makes the feedstock strategy survivable.

My 2008 thesis spent most of its length on supercritical methanol, which needs no catalyst and tolerates poor feedstock. It is genuinely elegant. It also runs at roughly 350 °C and 20 MPa, which at 3,000 t/yr means pressure vessels and an energy bill a cooperative cannot carry. Choosing the less interesting chemistry is the engineering decision.

Without that loop the methanol line would be roughly three times larger and the plant would not clear its costs.

Note what the glycerol line is doing. At €57k it is under 2% of revenue — but it is 13% of EBITDA. A by-product that looks negligible on the revenue statement is carrying an eighth of the return, which is exactly the kind of thing a feasibility study misses when it reads only the top line.

What the answer actually depends on

One variable dominates, and it is not the chemistry.

A single 20% rise in delivered oil price takes €354k out of a €433k EBITDA. The plant survives that on its own, and it survives a 10% fall in biodiesel price on its own. It does not survive both at once — that combination puts the project €206k under water, and the two are correlated, because the same energy-market conditions that depress fuel prices tend to loosen oil demand upstream.

So the engineering conclusion is blunt: this is not a process design problem, it is a procurement problem. The reactor configuration is settled, well understood and barely affects the outcome. What decides whether the plant is viable is whether the cooperative can lock in used cooking oil on multi-year terms at a known price. I would not recommend building this plant without that contract in hand, however good the process looks on paper.

That is also why the collection radius in the first figure is drawn at 60 km rather than 200. A wider catchment gets more oil but puts the plant in competition with buyers who can pay more, and turns a contractable local supply into a spot-priced one.

Risks worth stating plainly

The ones that would actually stop the project.
RiskWhy it matters hereResponse
Subsidised fossil diesel Iranian diesel is priced well below international levels. A biodiesel plant cannot win on price against a subsidised competitor in the open road-fuel market. Target off-road, marine, generator and export markets rather than domestic road fuel; treat any blending mandate as upside, not as the base case.
Feedstock competition Used cooking oil becomes valuable the moment someone else wants it. Informal collection networks can be outbid overnight. Multi-year supply agreements with hotel groups and processors before financial close; own the collection logistics rather than buying at the gate.
Feedstock seasonality Coastal tourism is strongly seasonal, so UCO volume swings through the year while the plant wants steady feed. Rapeseed as the counter-seasonal 20%; storage sized for roughly six weeks of feed.
Glycerol market depth 300 t/yr of crude glycerol is small, and the regional market for it is thin. Losing this line costs 13% of EBITDA. Offtake agreed with regional soap and animal-feed producers before commissioning; refining to technical grade only if a buyer justifies it.
Cold-flow properties UCO-derived FAME gels in cold weather. The coast is mild, but inland and mid-winter are not. Blend limits by season and additive package; specify B20 rather than B100 for winter supply.
Effluent handling 620 t/yr of wash effluent carrying soap and residual methanol is a permitting issue, not a rounding error. On-site treatment included in capex; dry-wash ion exchange as an alternative if discharge consent proves difficult.

From 2008 to now

What sixteen years changes about how you approach the same problem.

The 2008 thesis was not wrong. Its chemistry was sound, its literature review was thorough, and its treatment of reaction mechanisms would still hold up. What it lacked was any notion that an engineering answer has to be bought by someone. It compared catalytic routes on conversion efficiency and concluded that supercritical methanol was the most promising — which is true on the axis it measured, and irrelevant on the axis that decides whether anything gets built.

The work I have done since — maintenance strategy for an acid plant, fleet sizing for an offshore field, a 10 MW solar farm that had to clear a 27% IRR before anyone would sign — taught me to run the question the other way round. Start from who is paying, what they can bear, and which single variable moves the answer. Then choose the process that survives that variable, even when a more elegant one exists.

That is the whole difference between the two versions of this project. Same feedstock, same reaction, same country. One is a study of transesterification. The other is a plant somebody could decide to build, and a clear statement of the one contract they would need to sign first.
