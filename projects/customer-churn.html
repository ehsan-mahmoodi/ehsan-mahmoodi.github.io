Predictive analytics case study

Finding the Customers About to Leave — Before They Do

Asiatech had more than two million customers and a retention team that could only call so many of them. This is the path from anonymised behaviour data to a ranked list of the customers most likely to leave — segmentation, rule-based scores, classifiers and time-to-churn models combined into one ensemble — and the phone calls and offers that kept them.

Stakeholder engagement · business problem

Retention needs a list, not a dashboard

2020, Asiatech.

A customer who has already cancelled is expensive to win back. A customer who is about to cancel can often be kept with a phone call or a targeted offer — but only if someone knows who they are in time, and the retention budget is not spread across people who were never going to leave. The business question was therefore narrow and practical: of more than two million customers, who is most likely to leave next, and in what order should the retention team contact them?

The customer base was not one population. Alongside individual subscribers it included large business accounts — banks, insurance companies and government bodies — whose decisions follow contracts, procurement cycles and internal politics rather than the usage patterns that give an individual’s intentions away.

Data & privacy · descriptive analysis

Behaviour data, anonymised before it was modelled

SQL extraction from the company’s own databases into Python.

Customer behaviour and account history were pulled from the company’s databases with SQL and anonymised before any modelling: the models needed to know how a customer behaved, never who they were. Descriptive analysis in pandas established what normal behaviour looks like for different kinds of customer before any model was asked to spot the abnormal.

Diagnostic analysis

Segment first, then score

Customer segmentation and the business’s own warning signs, made explicit.

Segmentation grouped customers who behave alike, so that each group could be judged against its own normal rather than against a company-wide average that fits nobody. Alongside it, rule-based risk scores turned what the business already knew about customers who leave into explicit, checkable rules — a transparent baseline the statistical models had to beat, and one the retention team could understand without a data scientist in the room.

Predictive modelling

Six models, combined into one ensemble

Four model families, because no single one saw the whole picture.
  • Classifiers (scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM) answer the yes/no question: is this customer likely to churn?
  • Time-to-churn models answer the question the call centre actually needs: how soon? A customer likely to leave next month outranks one likely to leave next year.
  • Segmentation and rule-based scores carry the structure and the business knowledge into the ensemble rather than leaving them outside it.

The six models were combined into an ensemble, so that one model’s blind spot is covered by another’s strength, and were checked against historical outcomes — customers whose stay-or-leave decision was already known — before the scores were used to spend retention budget.

Prescriptive · action

From a score to a phone call

A ranked list, delivered where the retention team already worked.

The output was a ranked list of high-risk customers, served to the retention team through Power BI. They contacted those customers by phone and offered promotions or other incentives to stay — spending the retention budget on the customers most likely to leave, instead of on everyone.

Churn among individual customers fell by 20%. The honest limit is the business accounts: a bank or a government body does not leave because its usage pattern shifted, and behaviour data alone predicted them far less well. Stating that plainly is part of the result — it tells the business where a model is worth trusting and where an account manager’s relationship still matters more.
