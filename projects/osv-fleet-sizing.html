Simulation & Optimization Case Study

How Many Supply Vessels Does an Offshore Field Actually Need?

Offshore oil logistics comes down to the same decision everywhere: hire supply vessels long-term or short-term, size that fleet correctly, and pick a routing strategy — and the strategy choice alone can move total cost more than most people expect. Here is a real instance of it: four oil platforms in the Persian Gulf, one onshore base, and twenty vessels available to hire. My 2012 MSc thesis put a real operator's routing to the test and answered it with a validated discrete-event simulation, not a rule of thumb. The two papers that came out of it are the evidence; this page is the plain-English version, with the numbers the papers actually reported.

What this is. A published, peer-reviewed study, not a hypothetical demo. The simulation model was built on real operating data from the offshore field operator, from 2008–2011, its logic verified against expert review of the model animation, and its output validated with a paired t-test against real spot-hire-day records (t = 1.44, under the critical value of 2.07 — no statistically significant difference from reality). Every figure below is the paper's own result, not a reconstruction.

The problem: paying for a fleet you can't see the shape of

Four fields, one base, and a hiring decision made mostly on habit.

The operator's Kharg district serves four offshore production fields — Doroud, Forouzan, Esfandiar and Aboozar — from a single onshore base on Kharg Island, 57 km off the Bushehr coast. Each field generates two kinds of demand: delivery (food, water, fuel, equipment) and pickup (empty containers, waste, returned equipment). Missing a delivery risks a stalled platform; over-supplying the fleet just burns charter budget that never comes back.

Vessels come in two flavours. Long-term contracted OSVs cost roughly $2,000–4,000 per day and are committed capacity whether or not they're busy. Spot vessels cost $6,000–10,000 per day and are hired only when the long-term fleet can't cover demand. At the time of the study, the operator assigned vessels to a fixed weekly schedule regardless of how much each platform actually needed that week — the routing equivalent of a rota, not a dispatch decision.

Two routing policies, tested head to head

Same fields, same vessels, same demand — only the dispatch logic changes.
PolicyLogicWho used it
RP1 — Fixed schedule Each vessel has a predetermined weekly departure and route, regardless of that week's actual platform demand. The operator's actual practice at the time
RP2 — Demand-based Assign each request to the smallest vessel that can cover it, prefer a long-term vessel already available, and combine requests onto one vessel where possible. The policy this study proposes

To make the comparison fair, both policies were run through the same simulation-based optimizer, so neither is being judged on a lucky guess at fleet size. Each policy gets its own near-optimal fleet size and composition before the two are compared.

Model and method

Discrete-event simulation, calibrated to real conditions, tuned by simulation-optimization.
  • Platform demand distributions fit to the operator's historical pickup/delivery records per field.
  • Sailing speed scaled by a weather factor α (0.75/0.90/1.00) tied to significant wave height, so bad-weather delay is part of the model, not an afterthought.
  • Vessel pool of 20 candidates, each with its own speed (10–15 knots), capacity (70–100 containers) and day-rate — long-term $2,000–4,000/day, spot $6,000–10,000/day.
  • OptQuest-driven simulation optimization searches the 220 long-term hire/no-hire combinations for the near-optimal fleet at each of five target service levels (75/80/85/90/95%).
  • Validated against real 2009–2011 spot-hire-day counts with a paired t-test: no statistically significant difference between simulated and real operations.

Table 6 of the paper also breaks the saving down by cost type: RP1 leans on more long-term hiring (more fixed capital committed), while RP2 leans slightly more on short-term spot hire — but needs far fewer total vessel-days overall, so the net cost is still lower. Average platform waiting time is shorter under RP2 at every service level too; the saving is not bought with worse service, it comes with it.

Why the advantage grows exactly when it matters

This is not a fixed markup — it scales with how tightly you have to guarantee delivery.

At the loosest service target tested (75%), demand-based routing saves about 4% of fleet cost. Push the requirement to 95% — the level an operator actually wants once a stalled platform starts costing real production time — and the saving grows to 5.4%, because the fixed schedule has to over-provision broadly to hit high reliability everywhere, while demand-based routing only adds capacity where the demand actually is.

The practical read for a fleet manager: the fixed weekly rota is not wrong because it is unsophisticated, it is wrong because it prices in reliability it isn't using efficiently. A routing rule that reacts to platform requests, evaluated properly with simulation-optimization rather than a spreadsheet rule of thumb, recovers that inefficiency as cash — consistently, year on year, at zero capital cost.

From a 2012 thesis to two published papers

The work behind this page.

The routing formulation — a pickup-and-delivery vehicle routing problem with soft time windows and no ability to stage containers at sea — was published first, in the International Journal of Industrial Engineering & Production Management (2014). The fleet-sizing comparison on this page, with the validated simulation and the cost figures above, followed in Maritime Economics & Logistics (2016), a Palgrave/Macmillan journal. Together the two papers have collected 25 citations.
